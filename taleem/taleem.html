Getting ready

Attestation

Your certificates have to be verified by the UAE Embassy and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs before they count here. It's the longest task in the whole process, and it's the one most likely to delay your start.

Begin the day you accept

Attestation routinely takes six weeks or more depending on your country. Your visa can't be processed without it, and you can't sponsor your family without it either.

Which documents

  • Your degree and teaching qualification
  • Your marriage certificate
  • Birth certificates for your children
  • A name change certificate, if applicable

Family documents need attesting a second time by the UAE Embassy in order for you to sponsor your dependents. Sponsorship is your responsibility and is done at your own cost — your HR representative will guide you through it.

Attesting before you come

  1. Have your certificates notarised by a solicitor
  2. UK certificates only: legalised by the FCO
  3. Attested by the UAE Embassy in the country the certificates were issued
  4. Bring the documents or copies to the UAE and submit them to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs for final attestation

Attesting once you're here

  1. Take the documents to your home country's embassy for authentication
  2. Take them to the Department of Foreign Affairs to verify authenticity
  3. Take them to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Dubai for final attestation

Alternatively, you can send your documents back to your home country and have the whole process completed there.

Visas and sponsorship

Your HR representative handles the entire visa process for you. This page explains what's happening, and the two points where you have to turn up in person.

Working in the UAE requires an employment visa for the whole of your employment. Once you arrive you have a 30-day window to complete it, and HR will move it along within that time.

How it runs

  1. Your entry permit is processed and emailed to you
  2. You arrive and your status is changed
  3. You attend a medical — blood test and x-ray, or blood test only if you've lived in Dubai before
  4. You attend an Emirates ID centre for biometrics and an eye scan, which you may be able to skip if you've lived in the UAE before
  5. You return all documents and your passport to HR, who complete the process
  6. Your Emirates ID is issued and handed to you once it reaches the school

If your passport ever changes, tell HR immediately. Your visa is linked to your Emirates ID, and out-of-date passport details in the immigration system cause problems when you travel.

About the UAE

A federation of seven emirates on the eastern side of the Arabian Peninsula, formed on 2 December 1971. Around 9.9 million people live here, and roughly 88 in every 100 are expatriates.

Language and money

Arabic is the official language, but Arabic and English are both official business languages and a great many others are spoken across the expatriate community. The currency is the Emirati Dirham (AED), divided into 100 fils, with notes from 5 to 1,000.

Climate

A desert climate: mild, pleasant winters and very hot summers, with humidity that makes the heat harder work than the number suggests.

You'll be arriving in August, which is the hottest month of the year. It's a lot on day one, and it does become normal.

Moving to the UAE

Culture and customs

The UAE welcomes newcomers, and it does so with clear expectations. Knowing them makes life here easier from the first week.

What matters here

Hospitality, tolerance, family cohesion and solidarity run through Emirati society, along with real pride in that heritage. After faith, family comes first — children are considered jewels and family structures are close-knit.

The country's roots are in trade, fishing and pearl diving, which was the main source of income for many well into the early twentieth century. That history sits alongside a strong present-day push in science, technology and innovation.

Ramadan

The ninth month of the Islamic calendar, observed as a month of fasting, prayer, reflection and community. The dates shift each year because they follow the lunar cycle.

Don't eat, drink or smoke in public during daylight hours. This applies to non-Muslims too, and it's taken seriously — doing so can attract a fine or a reprimand from the police. Restaurants stay open and serve as normal, and most businesses work shorter hours.

Food and drink

An enormous range of cuisines, and supermarkets that cater to a very diverse population. Imported products cost more; local produce is cheap and good. Pork isn't served in most restaurants — designated venues hold pork licences, and selected supermarkets have separately labelled areas.

Moving to the UAE

Code of conduct

These are the professional, behavioural and moral obligations every Taaleem employee commits to, towards students, parents, colleagues and UAE society. Read this one properly — breaches can result in penalties against you.

Appearance and behaviour

  • No tight or otherwise inappropriate clothing for a working environment
  • No clothing carrying text or images offensive to the religion and culture of the UAE
  • No verbal or physical violence with students, and no unjustified physical contact outside the functional requirements of teaching
  • No discussion of actions that contradict prevailing UAE customs and traditions
  • Nothing that hinders identification of staff during working hours, including face covering, other than medical masks or health reasons

Conversations with students

Don't engage students in conversation about sensitive topics — including human reproduction and homosexuality — where it doesn't serve a clear educational purpose within the curriculum agreed for your school.

Similarly, avoid religious topics that provoke division or fall outside the country's approved Islamic Studies curriculum, and avoid the internal or external policies of any country, along with rumours or unverified claims that could disturb public order.

Resources and conflicts of interest

  • No gifts from students or their families, on or off campus, and no favouritism or nepotism
  • No financial participation that could jeopardise the school's goals or activities
  • No use of work resources for personal purposes or personal gain, and no wasteful spending
  • No private lessons without the appropriate authorisation

Alcohol, smoking and substances

  • Never come to work under the influence of alcohol or drugs
  • No smoking on school campus or in the yards
  • No sharing of prohibited materials with colleagues or students

Confidentiality and social media

  • Don't publish photographs of the school, staff, students or parents without the prior consent of everyone involved
  • Don't spread rumours or negative comment through any form of electronic communication
  • Don't make public comments that detract from the reputation of colleagues, the Ministry of Education, or education generally — this applies outside working hours too
  • Don't share confidential or sensitive information with anyone not authorised to have it
  • Don't disclose curricula or examination content

Exploring the UAE

Dubai and Abu Dhabi get the attention, but the other five emirates are worth your weekends too. Here's a starting list.

Dubai

Burj Khalifa, the historic souks either side of the creek, Sky Dive Dubai, Global Village, Miracle Garden, and Ski Dubai when the heat wins. The Metro and hop-on buses cover a lot of ground.

Abu Dhabi

Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque, Louvre Abu Dhabi, Emirates Palace and the Corniche. Yas Island holds Ferrari World, Warner Bros World and Yas Waterworld.

Ras Al Khaimah

Mountains, rock climbing and camping, plus the world's longest zipline at Jebel Jais.

Fujairah

On the Gulf of Oman coast. Diving and snorkelling, Snoopy Island for coral, turtles and reef sharks, and the country's oldest castle.

Sharjah

The Khor Fakkan amphitheatre and waterfall, and a lot for families.

Ajman and Umm Al Quwain

The quieter end. Ajman Museum in a restored fort, Al Hamriya Beach, and mangroves and kite surfing at Umm Al Quwain.

Social life here can get expensive quickly if you let it. Most people find that after the first few months they settle into something a lot more sustainable.

Types of leave

Beyond your annual leave, which follows the school calendar, there are four other kinds you should know about.

Sick leave

Maximum 15 calendar days per year

Call your Line Manager. Don't email or message. Notification must be by phone, before the start of normal office hours, and no later than 6:30am on the first morning of absence.

  • A medical certificate is required from day three, and must be an official document from the Dubai or Abu Dhabi Health Authority, at your own expense
  • Under UAE labour law there's no paid sick leave during your probation period
  • Weekends immediately before or after a period of sick leave aren't counted — but if the absence runs across both sides of a weekend, those days do count, and the certificate must cover the whole period including the weekend

Compassionate leave

Maximum 5 working days per year

Taaleem understands that living away from home means a bereavement may require you to travel at short notice. Compassionate leave covers the death of a close family member — parent, sibling, spouse, child, grandparent, mother-in-law or father-in-law.

Emergency leave

Maximum 5 working days per year

For the hospitalisation of a dependent, or an embassy or government appointment that can only be taken during working hours. Both compassionate and emergency leave are approved by your Principal, following your school's procedure.

In a genuine emergency, contact someone first and sort the paperwork afterwards.

Well-being leave

1 working day per academic year

A paid day dedicated to your physical, mental and emotional health. It has to be taken on its own and can't be combined with other types of leave.

School inspections

KHDA in Dubai and ADEK in Abu Dhabi inspect schools rigorously. If you haven't been through one before, here's what actually happens.

The week itself

Schools get five days' notice. The inspection team arrives just before the school day on Monday and leaves on Thursday.

  • Lesson observations are a significant part of it, across a cross-section of teachers in all phases — but it's unlikely everyone will be observed
  • The observation schedule isn't shared in advance, and observations range from part of a lesson to a full one
  • Inspectors are looking at learning, teaching, attainment and progress, and also at learning support, behaviour, use of resources and provision for SEN students
  • Inspectors talk to groups of students, meet staff, the Governing Body and parents

What you need to have ready

Your planning file and relevant assessment data. That's it.

You won't be graded

Feedback to teachers after lessons is optional, and you won't receive a judgement grade or anything in writing. Inspections judge the school, not you individually.

Afterwards

The lead inspector gives school leaders an emerging picture at the end of each day, so the school can supply more evidence if needed. An oral report on Thursday sets out strengths and main areas for improvement. Judgements stay provisional and confidential until quality assurance is complete, and the final report is published on the regulator's website later in the academic year.

Working at Taaleem

Unemployment insurance

The ILOE scheme provides income if you lose your job for reasons other than resignation or disciplinary action. Subscription has been mandatory since 1 January 2023 under Federal Decree Law No. 13 of 2022.

You subscribe to this yourself

It's mandatory but it isn't automatic — it's not deducted through payroll and Taaleem doesn't enrol you. Sort it once your Emirates ID arrives.

What you'd receive

60% of your average basic salary over the six months before the job loss, for up to three months — provided you've been subscribed for at least twelve consecutive months.

To subscribe

You need your Emirates ID or Unified Number, and a valid UAE mobile number. The scheme is open to Emiratis and residents working in the private and federal government sectors.
